# Motivation, satisfaction, and voluntary return: source notes for Soltech

Research checked 28 September 2026. Scope: a beginner crypto checker/scanner that people can enjoy without pressure to trade. These are eight original empirical papers, not a systematic review. Findings describe the studied populations and tasks; the proposed Soltech features and experiments are design inferences.

The defensible objective is a useful, understandable visit that people are glad they made, followed by another visit when they have a reason. Session length, scan count, confidence, and trading volume cannot establish that objective by themselves. None of the papers below establishes that a particular Soltech feature will cause lasting retention. Evidence from games is informative about experience, but financial decisions require direct product testing.

## 1. Needs and enjoyment: Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski (2006)

**Paper:** *The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach*. Motivation and Emotion, 30, 344–360.

- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8)
- [Original paper, SDT research archive](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf)

**Design/sample:** Three laboratory studies of commercial games (reported samples 89, 50, and 58 undergraduates) and an online survey of 730 MMO players.

**Finding:** Perceived autonomy and competence related to enjoyment and game preference. In the MMO survey, autonomy, competence, and relatedness each contributed to enjoyment and intended future play.

**Limit:** Future play was intention, not observed retention. Need satisfaction was measured, not independently randomized. Laboratory well-being effects were short term and mixed; the MMO sample was predominantly male. This is not evidence about a generation or crypto novices.

**Soltech inference:** Let users choose a purpose and depth, show understandable reasons for a result, and offer optional human help. Competence should mean interpreting evidence and uncertainty accurately, not simply feeling more confident.

## 2. Continuance beyond initial adoption: Bhattacherjee (2001)

**Paper:** *Understanding Information Systems Continuance: An Expectation-Confirmation Model*. MIS Quarterly, 25(3), 351–370.

- [Publisher/DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3250921)
- [Original article PDF, teaching copy](https://eli.johogo.com/Class/p3.pdf)

**Design/sample:** One-wave field survey of an online bank's customers; 122 usable responses from 1,000 randomly selected invitees, approximately 12% response. Accounts had been held for two months to three years.

**Finding:** Satisfaction and perceived usefulness were associated with intention to continue using online banking. Confirmation of expectations related to satisfaction and subsequent usefulness perceptions.

**Limit:** A cross-sectional structural model cannot establish temporal causality. Low response, one provider, self-reports, and intention rather than subsequent behavior constrain generalization. Account tenure does not make this a longitudinal study.

**Soltech inference:** Make an accurate first-use promise, then deliver it: understand the available evidence, unresolved questions, and what changed. Do not let a polished presentation imply comprehensive protection or predictive certainty.

## 3. Activity versus intrinsic motivation: Mekler et al. (2017)

**Paper:** *Towards understanding the effects of individual gamification elements on intrinsic motivation and performance*. Computers in Human Behavior, 71, 525–534; online publication originated in 2015.

- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048)
- [University of Basel article record and abstract](https://edoc.unibas.ch/entities/publication/ccec8ccc-aa54-417d-b205-334b518d6eed)
- [First author's dissertation containing the study; sample verified in indexed text](https://edoc.unibas.ch/44253/1/diss_emekler_ub_noCV.pdf)

**Design/sample:** Online image-annotation experiment, N=273; four interface conditions crossed analytically with measured autonomy/control causality orientation.

**Finding:** Points, levels, and a leaderboard increased annotation quantity relative to control, without significant intrinsic-motivation or competence gains. Quantity did not demonstrate better experience.

**Limit:** A brief task, specific implementations, and measured individual orientation. Null effects do not prove all gamification ineffective or inherently harmful; long-term return was not tested.

**Soltech inference:** Do not treat scans, refreshes, or badges earned as evidence of satisfaction. If progress is added, attach it to demonstrated understanding, with correctness and pressure measures alongside activity.

## 4. Different mechanics, different needs: Sailer et al. (2017)

**Paper:** *How gamification motivates: An experimental study of the effects of specific game design elements on psychological need satisfaction*. Computers in Human Behavior, 69, 371–380.

- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033)
- [Original article PDF](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/195975/mod_folder/content/0/Sailer%20et%20al_2017.pdf)

**Design/sample:** Random assignment to three warehouse-simulation conditions. 699 started; 419 completed; 331 analyzed after excluding 88 who failed recognition checks. All conditions included points.

**Finding:** Badges/leaderboard/performance graphs together improved competence and task meaningfulness; avatars/story/teammates together improved relatedness. Neither bundle improved decision freedom. Reported omnibus effects were small.

**Limit:** Bundles prevent attribution to one mechanic. Attrition and post-assignment exclusions weaken causal interpretation. Single simulation session; no retention outcome.

**Soltech inference:** An avatar picker alone is not meaningful autonomy. Prefer consequential choices, such as which evidence to inspect. Test an optional explanation-sharing or help feature separately before assuming users want a social feed or competition.

## 5. Habit formation without a universal deadline: Lally et al. (2010)

**Paper:** *How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world*. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40, 998–1009; first published online 2009.

- [Publisher original abstract and methods summary](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

**Design/sample:** 96 volunteers selected a daily eating, drinking, or activity behavior in a stable context over 12 weeks. 82 supplied sufficient data; curves fitted 62; 39 had good model fit.

**Finding:** Self-reported automaticity developed gradually and varied substantially. Estimated time to 95% of the fitted asymptote ranged 18–254 days. Missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt the process.

**Limit:** Self-selected health behaviors, self-report, no randomized app intervention. Some duration estimates extrapolated beyond the 84-day observation period. This does not establish a 21- or 66-day retention rule.

**Soltech inference:** Support an optional, user-chosen review routine and forgiving resumption. Do not prescribe daily market checking or reset useful learning progress after an absence.

## 6. Notification timing and control: Fitz et al. (2019)

**Paper:** *Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being*. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84–94.

- [Publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563219302596)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)
- [Original article PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57a40c19414fb54f51f8095f/t/614a55faa7b89e25f4e48ad1/1632261627146/2019%2BFitz%2BBatching.pdf)

**Design/sample:** Randomized field experiment, N=237 smartphone users recruited through MTurk; one baseline week and two intervention weeks. Conditions: usual delivery, hourly batches, three daily batches, or none.

**Finding:** Three daily batches improved reported concentration and control versus usual delivery. Blanket suppression increased anxiety and phone-related FoMO. Batching also had missed-notification concerns; benefits were not universal.

**Limit:** Broad phone notifications, restricted settings, short duration, imperfect usage data, and multiple comparisons without adjustment. Some article wording contradicts other passages on stress/productivity; those directions should not be quoted uncritically. No optimal Soltech frequency is established.

**Soltech inference:** Offer opt-in, predictable summaries, visible scheduling, pause, and easy adjustment. Do not translate this into three daily market alerts or assume notifications-off is harmful in Soltech.

## 7. Hedonic and pragmatic experience: Hassenzahl, Diefenbach, and Göritz (2010)

**Paper:** *Needs, affect, and interactive products – Facets of user experience*. Interacting with Computers, 22(5), 353–362.

- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.002)
- [Original article PDF](https://assets.website-files.com/6130bc5b67f7afc3311fd697/61ecc672e7e91dceae8bb1cd_Needs%2C%20affect%2C%20and%20interactive%20products%20%281%29.pdf)

**Design/sample:** German-language online recall survey. 548 of 668 participants completed most key measures; median age 32. Participants described an outstanding positive technology experience.

**Finding:** Need fulfillment correlated with positive affect and hedonic product qualities more strongly than pragmatic qualities. Whether people credited the product for the experience mattered.

**Limit:** Retrospective, selected positive episodes; no experiment, ordinary negative experiences, or long-term retention measurement. The paper does not show that usability is unimportant, that beauty causes trustworthiness, or that decoration satisfies psychological needs.

**Soltech inference:** Pair usability with a satisfying outcome: a clear explanation, a learned distinction, or a well-organized personal watchlist. Test visual appeal separately from task comprehension and evidence quality.

## 8. Measuring experience, not merely clicks: O'Brien, Cairns, and Hall (2018)

**Paper:** *A practical approach to measuring user engagement with the refined user engagement scale (UES) and new UES short form*. International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 112, 28–39.

- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2018.01.004)
- [University repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/129012/)
- [Original paper PDF, Open University repository](https://oro.open.ac.uk/68806/1/1-s2.0-S1071581918300041-main.pdf)

**Design/sample:** Psychometric reanalysis of 427 and 779 valid responses, plus digital-library data from 344 participants.

**Finding:** Supports a four-factor structure—focused attention, perceived usability, aesthetic appeal, and reward—and a 12-item short form with three items per factor.

**Limit:** A validated engagement measure is not a measure of financial safety or observed retention. Context still matters. Reward here describes the experience, not tokens or prizes.

**Soltech inference:** Use the published scoring and disclose adaptations. Report subscales alongside comprehension and return behavior. Do not maximize losing track of time as the goal of a quick checker; altered or partial questionnaires must not be presented as the validated full scale.

## Product hypotheses worth testing

The following are proposed Soltech experiments, not effects established by the cited papers. They intentionally treat choosing to stop or deciding against a trade as compatible with a successful visit.

| Hypothesis and change | Comparison and falsifiable outcomes | Evidence motivation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaningful choice improves autonomy without obstructing the task. Offer “Check this token,” “Understand a result,” or “Review my watchlist”; allow a quick default and change later. | Randomize the choice entry against the existing entry. Measure task completion, self-reported agency, unnecessary navigation, and week-4 useful return. Reject the product hypothesis if choice adds friction without a meaningful agency or return gain. | Ryan; Sailer |
| A concise rationale improves competence and satisfaction. Explain a finding, its source/time, and one uncertainty; allow expansion. | Use identical underlying data. Test factual interpretation and recognition of what cannot be concluded, plus satisfaction immediately and at next visit. Reject if confidence rises while accuracy does not or if users infer safety guarantees. | Ryan; Bhattacherjee |
| A clear completion point creates a satisfying visit. End with a short recap, unresolved questions, and optional save-for-later. | Compare with the same content without a recap. Measure successful completion, perceived worth, repeat refreshes with no new data, and later useful return. Shorter sessions with stable or better outcomes count as success. | Bhattacherjee; Hassenzahl |
| Persistent learning progress is useful without attendance pressure. Show concepts understood, no streak reset or trade-based rewards. | Compare educational progress feedback with the same lessons and no progress display. Measure delayed transfer on new examples, enjoyment, return after an absence, and felt obligation. Reject if it mainly increases clicks or scan count. | Mekler; Lally |
| A predictable optional summary supports chosen return. Offer a user-controlled schedule and an on-demand equivalent. | Among consenting reminder users, compare schedule options while preserving pause/override. Measure relevance, interruption burden, notification disabling, FoMO/pressure, and useful return. No universal frequency is presumed. | Fitz |
| Optional help adds relatedness without social pressure. Provide a route to ask a question or share an explanation privately. | Start with a small prototype study before building a community. Check actual demand, comprehension after help, comfort asking, and perceived comparison pressure. Reject if novice users feel exposed or prefer self-service. | Ryan; Sailer |
| A calmer, polished presentation adds enjoyment while retaining legibility. | Compare visual treatments using identical wording, evidence, and task paths. Measure aesthetic appeal, comprehension, information-finding time, and satisfaction again after several visits. A first-impression preference alone is insufficient. | Hassenzahl; O'Brien |

## A measurement plan that can disconfirm the proposal

Use per-user random assignment for behavioral experiments, with a persistent holdout. Start with moderated beginner tasks to find misunderstandings; these sessions are diagnostic, not a statistical estimate of retention. Run a subsequent 4–6 week field test across changing market conditions, and reserve claims of durable effects for longer follow-up. A 4–6 week duration is an initial study choice, not a proven threshold.

Predefine one primary outcome and the smallest useful effect for each experiment before collecting results. Set sample size from baseline rates and a power analysis, and report uncertainty, missing responses, allocation, and attrition. Analyze everyone by assigned condition where possible; do not remove users simply because they ignored a new feature. Simultaneous variants need the same market data and timestamps so price conditions do not masquerade as a UI effect.

Proposed outcome definitions:

- **Useful return:** a later visit containing a user-initiated evidence review, a completed comprehension task, or a saved-item review that the user rates useful. Validate these event proxies with interviews before making them a score.
- **Voluntary return:** distinguish direct visits from notification/reward-linked visits in logs, and sample users' stated reasons. Absence of a recent push is only an operational proxy; it does not prove intrinsic motivation.
- **Satisfaction:** evaluate whether the visit felt worthwhile and achieved the user's purpose. Pair immediate reports with delayed reports after several visits to expose novelty effects.
- **Competence:** use fresh scenarios, uncertainty recognition, and confidence calibration. Confidence alone is insufficient.
- **Autonomy and pressure:** use short exploratory items about acting by choice, feeling able to stop, and feeling pushed to trade or keep checking. Label new/adapted items as unvalidated and report them separately.
- **Guardrails:** misunderstanding a risk label, unsupported safety inference, increased pressure/FoMO, interruption burden, and repeated refreshing without changed evidence. Do not declare a variant successful solely because return rose while these worsened.

Do not infer demographic preferences from labels such as “Gen Z.” Recruit across relevant experience, confidence, device, accessibility needs, and stated purpose; test demographic differences only with adequate sampling. No dopamine explanation is needed for any proposal here.

## Source verification notes

Dates above follow journal publication years, with earlier online dates stated where useful. Source finding summaries are deliberately short. Full original paper content was consulted where accessible; publisher and university records were used for bibliographic confirmation. Bhattacherjee and some other papers are available as copies of the original article on teaching/research sites; these links are access copies, not independent replications. No claim of a systematic literature search, exhaustive consensus, financial outcome validation, or measured Soltech benefit is made.
